Page One · The Big Story

Curaleaf's Boris Jordan Vows to “Substantially” Raise Its Aurora Bid — but Only With Due-Diligence Access — as Green Thumb Beats on Revenue, Trulieve Misses, and the Sector Gives Back Nearly 4%

A one-day-old agreed deal between Aurora and Organigram is not as settled as Wednesday's market action suggested; a split earnings day adds more noise than signal for the sector tape.

Curaleaf chairman and CEO Boris Jordan told analysts on the company's Nov. 3 third-quarter earnings call that Curaleaf intends to "improve our bid substantially" for Aurora Cannabis, a day after Aurora signed a definitive C$7.80-per-share arrangement agreement with BAT-backed Organigram Global — remarks that only gained wide pickup Nov. 4–5 as wire aggregators and Canadian outlets caught up to the call transcript. Jordan declined to name a price and called full due-diligence access, which Aurora has denied Curaleaf while granting it to Organigram, an "absolute condition" before Curaleaf would materially raise its offer. He argued Organigram's bid is "just under 10% better" than Curaleaf's own (implying roughly a 21% gap versus Curaleaf's value at Monday's close), characterized Organigram's financing as still contingent, and said Aurora is "a bonus rather than a necessity" for Curaleaf given its own record $353 million Q3. Curaleaf's current offer — $1.00 cash plus 0.4013 Curaleaf shares per Aurora share, capped at $6.00 — stands unchanged and remains open through its Dec. 4, 11:59pm MT deadline; no revised terms have been filed with any regulator as of this edition. (The Deep Dive; SEC 6-K filings.)

Separately, newly reviewed SEC exhibits confirm the Organigram-Aurora arrangement's break-fee architecture: Aurora owes Organigram a C$25.2 million termination fee if the deal fails on a shareholder vote or an Aurora-side breach, while Organigram owes Aurora a larger C$38.4 million reverse termination fee (plus a C$5.6 million expense-reimbursement fee) under mirrored failure conditions. On the earnings side, Green Thumb Industries posted Q3 revenue of $324.4 million (+11.3% year-over-year, beating a $313.2 million estimate) but a GAAP net loss of $4.8 million (-$0.02/share, missing a $0.05 estimate), alongside its first positive same-store sales growth (+3.0%) since Q3 2024; Roth Capital trimmed its price target to $18 from $20 while keeping a Buy. Trulieve's Q3 print ran the opposite direction: revenue of $230.2 million missed a $273.7 million estimate and fell 20% year-over-year, but adjusted EPS of $0.09 beat, net income came in positive at $1 million, and the company confirmed it has received final federal DEA registrations under the Schedule III order. MSOS gave back roughly 4% on the session, with TerrAscend and Planet 13 still to report after today's close.

Why it matters to PGP Jordan's comments reframe Wednesday's assumption — echoed by TD Cowen's Sell-and-tender call — that the Organigram deal is effectively done. A credible strategic bidder publicly conditioning a "substantial" raise on diligence access, rather than walking away, is a live threat to deal certainty that PGP should price into any Organigram/Aurora-adjacent work, including the asymmetric break-fee structure itself: a larger reverse fee protects Aurora's board from exactly the scenario Jordan is now describing — an outbid, undiligenced strategic refusing to go away. Separately, Green Thumb's revenue-beat/EPS-miss and Trulieve's revenue-miss/EPS-beat are a reminder that rescheduling-era cost structures (DEA registration costs, 280E relief timing) are moving through MSO income statements unevenly — comp-level diligence should not assume a uniform read-through from one operator's earnings quality to another's.

Why it matters to PGP Jordan's public, on-the-record conditioning of a "substantial" bid raise on diligence access is a negotiating tactic PGP should recognize from both sides of a topping fight: it keeps Curaleaf's tender technically alive through Dec. 4 without committing capital, while pressuring Aurora's board to either grant access (reopening the contest) or hold firm and risk a public "we were never really tested" narrative if Organigram's deal closes uncontested. The disclosed break-fee asymmetry (a larger reverse fee than forward fee) remains a useful template for deals where a strategic investor is underwriting financing risk — PGP should treat Jordan's comments as evidence that board-level diligence-access decisions are now a disclosed-adjacent variable in how rival bidders price their next move, not just a private boardroom matter.
