Page One · The Big Story

Massachusetts Crushes the Nation's First Legalization-Repeal Vote, 76%–24%, as Curaleaf Fires Back at Aurora's Board — Calling the Organigram Deal a “Fiduciary Breach” — While TD Cowen Downgrades Aurora to Sell and Tells Holders to Tender Anyway

A landslide "no" in Boston removes the sector's biggest political overhang of the year; in Canada, the Aurora fight gets uglier rather than resolved, even as the market quietly treats it as settled.

Massachusetts voters rejected Question 8 — the measure to repeal the state's decade-old adult-use cannabis law — by roughly 76% to 24%, according to unofficial Associated Press results with 86–90% of precincts counted as of Wednesday morning. The margin blew past even the most favorable pre-election polling (61%–71% opposed) and makes Massachusetts the first state in the country to decisively defeat an organized ballot effort to undo voter-approved legalization, a decade after the original 2016 vote passed with 54% support. Had it passed, retail shops would have faced closure by 2028 and home cultivation would have been banned; instead, the state's adult-use market continues operating exactly as before. (STAT News; Marijuana Moment; NORML; Cannabis Business Times.)

In Canada, the Aurora Cannabis fight did not go quiet the way it did after past hostile-bid rounds. Hours after Organigram's C$561 million agreed deal was announced Tuesday, Curaleaf issued a same-day statement — not silence, as this desk's prior edition left open — accusing Aurora's board of "gross dereliction of their fiduciary duties" for granting Organigram due-diligence access it denied Curaleaf, and explicitly declining to withdraw or improve its own now-outbid tender, which it says "remains outstanding" through its Dec. 4, 11:59pm MT deadline. Curaleaf argues its own offer delivers better value, more deal certainty and larger synergies, and says it is still reviewing the unfiled details of Organigram's termination-fee structure. TD Cowen, meanwhile, downgraded Aurora to Sell from Buy on Wednesday — not because it dislikes the deal, but because it reads the outcome as settled and is telling shareholders the "cleaner path to value realization" is simply to tender into Organigram's offer rather than hold for upside. (PR Newswire/TradingView; The Marijuana Herald; Investing.com; MarketBeat.)

Why it matters to PGP Massachusetts' margin is the clearest evidence yet that post-rescheduling political risk to adult-use markets is smaller than the sector has priced — a repeal campaign backed by national prohibitionist money (Smart Approaches to Marijuana Action) lost by more than 3-to-1 in a swing-ish electorate, which should raise PGP's confidence in state-legalization durability as a diligence input. Separately, TD Cowen's "Sell the stock, tender the shares" call is a useful signal of how sophisticated capital is now pricing contested cannabis M&A: once a strategic-backed topping bid is signed, the market treats the outcome as a probability-weighted arbitrage, not a re-rating catalyst — exactly the framing PGP should bring to any client sitting on the other side of a late, unmatched hostile bid.

Why it matters to PGP The disclosed Organigram/Aurora break-fee structure — a larger reverse fee ($38.4M) than forward fee ($25.2M) — is a useful template for PGP: it tilts economic risk toward the acquirer backing out, which is exactly the right asymmetry when a strategic investor (BAT) is underwriting the financing and the target's board needs real downside protection against a financing deal falling through. Separately, resolving the Premier Group/PCF date error is itself the lesson: a registry that never updates is not evidence of a live, slow-moving transaction — it can just as easily mean the underlying deal is already over a year old.
